In the early 19th century the Frenchman **Joseph Fourier**, studying how heat spreads, had an idea that seemed crazy:
**any periodic wave, even one with sharp corners, can be written as a sum of sine waves** of different frequencies.

Below, each circle is a wave: it turns at a certain speed and has a certain radius. Stacking the circles one on top of another, the tip of the last one draws the sum.
With a single wave you get a smooth sine curve; adding the frequencies 3, 5, 7… with ever smaller radii, the curve becomes almost a **square wave**.

<Interattivo tipo="fourier" titolo="Fourier’s circles" didascalia="Each circle is a sine wave. Add waves with the slider and watch the curve turn square." altezza={340} />

<Esempio titolo="Where you use it every day">
**Wi-Fi** and 4G/5G split data across many neighbouring waves and separate them with the Fourier transform; **MP3s** and **JPEG** photos throw away the waves the ear or eye doesn’t notice;
music **equalisers** raise or lower single frequencies. See also <a href="/en/matematica-ovunque/">Everything is maths</a>.
</Esempio>

<Curiosita titolo="Corners that won’t go away">
Near the jumps of the square wave the sum always “overshoots” a little past the edge, even with a great many waves: it’s called the **Gibbs phenomenon**. Try taking the slider to 25 and look at the corners.
</Curiosita>
