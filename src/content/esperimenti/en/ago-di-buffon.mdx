In the 18th century the French naturalist **Georges-Louis Leclerc, Comte de Buffon**, asked himself a question: if I drop a needle at random onto a floor
with parallel stripes, what is the probability that the needle touches a line? The experiment was described in **1777**.

## The (surprising) answer

If the needle has length $\ell$ and the lines are a distance $t$ apart, with $\ell \le t$, the probability that the needle touches a line is

$$
p = \frac{2\ell}{\pi\, t}
$$

**π** appears, even though there is no circle! The reason is that the needle can land at **any angle**, and angles “go round” as in a circle.
Turning the formula around, if you drop $n$ needles and $m$ of them touch a line:

$$
\pi \approx \frac{2\ell\, n}{t\, m}
$$

<Curiosita titolo="The Lazzarini case">
In 1901 the Italian teacher Mario Lazzarini reported that with 3408 drops he had obtained $\pi \approx 3.1415929$: exactly the fraction
$355/113$, correct to six decimal places. Too good to be true: with so few drops the typical error is around a hundredth.
In 1994 the mathematician Lee Badger showed that the result must have been “adjusted”, for example by stopping at just the right moment.
Others, such as the journalist Hans van Maanen in the magazine of CICAP (the Italian skeptics’ association), think rather of a joke played on fellow teachers.
Either way the lesson stands: in statistics, an experiment that is too perfect is a warning sign. Try it above: how many drops do you need for two correct decimals?
</Curiosita>

<Esempio titolo="Why it matters">
Buffon’s needle is one of the first examples of the **Monte Carlo method**: using chance, repeated a huge number of times, to calculate a number.
Today methods of this kind are used in physics, finance and meteorology.
</Esempio>
