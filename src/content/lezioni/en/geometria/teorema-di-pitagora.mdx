## The statement

In a **right-angled triangle** we call the two sides that form the right angle the **legs** and the opposite side, the longest one, the **hypotenuse**.
The theorem says:

$$
a^2 + b^2 = c^2
$$

where $a$ and $b$ are the legs and $c$ is the hypotenuse. In pictures: **the area of the square on the hypotenuse equals the sum of the areas of the squares on the legs**.

## How to use it

**Finding the hypotenuse.** Legs 3 and 4: $c = \sqrt{3^2 + 4^2} = \sqrt{9 + 16} = \sqrt{25} = 5$.

**Finding a leg.** Hypotenuse 13 and leg 5: $b = \sqrt{13^2 - 5^2} = \sqrt{169 - 25} = \sqrt{144} = 12$.

<Esempio titolo="Worked example: the ladder against the wall">
A **5 m** ladder leans against a wall, with its foot **3 m** from the wall. How high does it reach?
The ladder is the hypotenuse: $h = \sqrt{5^2 - 3^2} = \sqrt{16} = 4$ m.
Builders do the same calculation to check that a corner is truly square: they mark 3 and 4 on the two sides and check that the diagonal measures 5.
</Esempio>

<Attenzione>
Common mistake: thinking that $\sqrt{a^2 + b^2} = a + b$. With 3 and 4 you’d get 7 instead of 5! The root of a sum is **not** the sum of the roots.
And careful: the theorem works **only** for right-angled triangles.
</Attenzione>

<Curiosita titolo="Pythagorean triples">
Three whole numbers satisfying $a^2 + b^2 = c^2$ are called a **Pythagorean triple**: (3, 4, 5), (5, 12, 13), (8, 15, 17)…
Multiply a triple by the same number and you get another one: (6, 8, 10) is (3, 4, 5) doubled.
</Curiosita>
