**Abu Ja'far Muhammad ibn Musa al-Khwarizmi** was born around 780 and died around 850. He worked in Baghdad at the **House of Wisdom**,
a great centre of learning where Greek scientific texts were translated and where people wrote about algebra, geometry and astronomy,
under the patronage of the caliph **al-Ma’mun**, to whom he dedicated two of his treatises.

## The book that gave algebra its name

His most famous treatise is *Hisab al-jabr w'al-muqabala*. The word **al-jabr** gave us **algebra**: many historians consider it the first book
devoted to this subject. Al-Khwarizmi explains how to solve linear and quadratic equations by reducing them to six standard forms,
using two operations:

- **al-jabr**, “completion”: removing negative terms (for example from $x^2 = 40x - 4x^2$ to $5x^2 = 40x$);
- **al-muqabala**, “balancing”: removing equal quantities present on both sides.

A curiosity: al-Khwarizmi used no symbols. He wrote everything **in words**: $x$ was “the root”, $x^2$ “the square”.

## From al-Khwarizmi to “algorithm”

He also wrote a treatise on **Indian numerals**, that is the place-value system with the digits 1 to 9 and 0. The Arabic original is lost,
but a Latin translation began with *Algoritmi de numero Indorum*: “Algoritmi” was his Latinised name. That’s where the word **algorithm** comes from.

## The journey to Italy

Centuries later, **Leonardo of Pisa**, known as Fibonacci, learned “the art of the nine Indian figures” as a boy, in Bugia, in North Africa.
In **1202** he published the *Liber abaci*, which spread the place-value system and the Arabic numerals across Europe. The ones you use today.
