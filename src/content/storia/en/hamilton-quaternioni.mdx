**William Rowan Hamilton** (Dublin, 1805–1865) was an Irish astronomer and mathematician. For years he had been looking for a way to do with
**points in three-dimensional space** what complex numbers do with points in the plane: add them, multiply them, divide them.
With “triples” of numbers he couldn’t manage it.

## The flash by the canal

On Monday **16 October 1843** he was walking with his wife along the Royal Canal, on his way to a meeting of the Royal Irish Academy.
She was talking to him, he was barely listening: the idea was taking shape. He needed **a fourth dimension**. As he later wrote, it was as if an electric
circuit closed and a spark flashed.

When he reached **Broom Bridge** (which he called Brougham Bridge), he couldn’t resist and carved the fundamental rules into the stone:

$$
i^2 = j^2 = k^2 = ijk = -1
$$

## What quaternions are

A quaternion is a number of the form $a + bi + cj + dk$, with three “imaginary units” $i, j, k$. The surprise: **multiplication is not commutative**.
$ij = k$ but $ji = -k$. It was the first non-commutative algebra to be studied, a revolutionary idea.

<Esempio titolo="Where you meet them today">
Quaternions are used to describe **rotations in space**: you find them, for example, in 3D graphics, video games,
robotics and in controlling the orientation of drones and satellites, because they avoid some of the problems of traditional angles.
</Esempio>

## The bridge today

Today there is a **commemorative plaque** with the formula on the bridge. Every year, on 16 October, mathematicians from all over the world
take part in the **Hamilton Walk**, from Dunsink Observatory to the bridge.

<Curiosita>
Hamilton was convinced that quaternions would be as important to the 19th century as calculus had been to the late 17th.
He spent the rest of his life on them: his *Elements of Quaternions* was meant to be 400 pages long and take 2 years. It came out twice as long, after seven years.
</Curiosita>
